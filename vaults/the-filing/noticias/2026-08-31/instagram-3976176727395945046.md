## Descrição

It was only 10:30 a.m. on a Tuesday morning, five days before the U.S. Open singles draw actually started, but spectators had packed the 14,000 seats of Louis Armstrong Stadium to the brim.⁠
⁠
They had come to see a 21-year-old Filipino tennis prodigy named Alex Eala, who was paired up with Canada’s Félix Auger-Aliassime. They greeted her with a roar, cheered every point, and screamed with such deafening volume that her opponents made a string of unforced errors in the middle of a game. Though the match only lasted roughly 39 minutes—and Eala and Auger-Aliassime lost—fans had gotten what they came for: a chance to see Eala in the flesh.⁠
⁠
Tennis is hardly a national pastime in the Philippines, ranking far behind basketball and soccer to the archipelago’s population of roughly 120 million. The country’s most famous athlete is, without a doubt, former world champion boxer Manny Pacquiao, who racked up titles in eight different weight classes before briefly serving as a senator. His name remains synonymous with national pride.⁠
⁠
Now, in the space of a season, Eala is primed to capture something similar.⁠
⁠
Those who come to watch her play, wave Filipino flags and wear custom T-shirts with her likeness on the front. In Manila, her face is plastered across a billboard on Epifanio de los Santos Avenue, the city’s longest stretch of highway.⁠
⁠
Earlier this month, at the DC Open, she delivered her true breakout. She toppled world No. 3 Jessica Pegula in three sets to win her first WTA tour title, becoming the first tennis player from the Philippines to win any professional tennis title.⁠
⁠
This time last year, she entered the U.S. Open as the world No. 75. This week, she arrived as the world No. 18, and seeded 17th at the tournament.⁠
